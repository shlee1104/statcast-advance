Sequencing

To lefties, the four-seam sets up the curveball: it follows 25% of the time, against his usual 18% (119 of 482).

Nothing stands out to this side: whatever he just threw, the next pitch follows his usual mix.

"n" is how many times the pitch was followed by another in the same plate appearance; "usual" is how often a pitch follows anything, to that side. A coloured line also has to stand clear of chance, and a pitch with fewer than 40 follow-ups sends out no lines. Across 58 pitcher-seasons, 9 in 10 coloured lines pointed the same way in the other half of the season and about 6 in 10 were as strong. The count is not held fixed: what a pitch does decides the count the next one is thrown in, so part of any line is ordinary count logic.

To righties, the cutter after a four-seam comes through the same window, and from there the two move 3 inches apart (57 pairs).

The follow-ups above, read for what a hitter can see. He has to decide when the ball is about 24 feet away. Each row is a pitch and the one that followed it: how far apart the two leave his hand, where the second is at the decision point relative to the first, and how differently it moves after that, which the hitter does not get to see before he commits. All three are averages of the difference, so where he happened to throw each pitch cancels out.

"Reads as" divides the offset at the decision point by how much his own pitches scatter there (4.6 inches to lefties, 4.7 to righties). Same window means where the ball is at that moment would pick the right pitch of the two less than 65% of the time; apart early means more than 73%. Between the two it gets no word. A pair is listed once it has come 30 times to that side.

How far to trust it: across 45 pitcher-seasons, 9 in 10 "same window" pairs stayed on that side of the line in the other half of the season and 7 in 10 earned the word again; for "apart early" it was 8 in 10 and under 6 in 10. The offset itself moves by a median of about an inch and a quarter between halves, which is why it is here and not on the card. Late movement and the gap out of the hand barely move at all. 23.8 feet is a convention: it is where an average fastball is about 175 ms from contact, and it is further from contact for a slow pitch, so this understates how readable a slow curveball is.
